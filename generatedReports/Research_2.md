# How do black holes form?

## Abstract
Black holes form through multiple astrophysical pathways that operate at different mass and time scales. Stellar-mass black holes arise from the gravitational collapse of massive stars at the end of their lives, with the minimum mass of a black hole formed by stellar collapse governed by the maximum neutron-star mass and believed to be about 2–4 M☉ [1]. Supermassive black hole (SMBH) seeds can form by the direct collapse of pristine massive gas clouds under conditions that suppress H2 cooling, producing seeds in the ≳10^4–10^6 M☉ range with high accretion rates of order 0.1–1 M☉ yr^−1 [9]. Simulations and semi-analytic models find direct-collapse pathways are rare but sufficient in some regions (abundance estimates of order 10^−4 Mpc^−3 are reported for certain scenarios) and are sensitive to radiative backgrounds and baryon–dark-matter streaming [9,5].

## Introduction
Black hole formation is not a single process but a set of channels determined by mass scale, environment, and epoch. At stellar scales, massive stars collapsing after supernovae create compact remnants that become black holes when pressure support can no longer resist gravity; the minimum mass for such remnants is thought to be roughly 2–4 M☉ [1]. At the high-mass end, SMBHs can grow via sustained high accretion or by forming massive seeds through direct collapse of gas in the early universe under special thermal and radiative conditions; such seeds are predicted in the ≳10^4 M☉ range and can form rapidly (timescales ∼10 Myr in some models) [9,5]. Additional, rarer pathways include primordial black holes formed in the early universe and hypothetical micro black holes from high-energy collisions that would evaporate extremely quickly (∼10^−25 s) if they existed [1].

## Stellar-collapse (Stellar-mass) Black Hole Formation
Stellar-mass black holes typically form when massive stars exhaust their nuclear fuel and undergo core collapse, often associated with a supernova event that leaves a compact remnant [1]. If the core remnant mass exceeds the maximum supportable mass for a neutron star (estimated to be about 2–4 M☉), it will continue collapsing to a black hole [1]. After formation, these black holes grow predominantly by accreting gas from their surroundings or by merging with other compact objects over time [1]. The stellar-collapse channel is the dominant formation route for black holes in the few to tens of solar-mass range observed in X-ray binaries and gravitational-wave detections [1].

## Direct Collapse and Supermassive Black Hole Seeds
Direct collapse is a proposed pathway in which pristine, massive gas clouds avoid fragmentation and collapse nearly isothermally into a single massive object, forming a SMBH seed rather than a cluster of stars [5,9]. A key requirement is suppression of H2 cooling (the main coolant in primordial gas); models report that the required Lyman–Werner flux threshold Jcrit can be very high (Jcrit,21 ≃ 10^2–10^5 in units of 10^−21 erg s^−1 cm^−2 Hz^−1 sr^−1), well above typical backgrounds, so direct collapse is expected to be rare and environmentally specific [9]. When suppression conditions hold, accretion rates of order 0.1–1 M☉ yr^−1 can build supermassive stars that collapse into seeds of ≳10^4 M☉ on relatively short timescales (∼10 Myr), and some models find comoving abundances sufficient to explain high-redshift SMBHs detected by JWST spectroscopy [9]. Bromm (2003) and subsequent simulations explore the parameter space of halo mass, cooling, and angular momentum transport needed for this pathway, emphasizing the sensitivity to early-universe conditions [5].

## Role of Baryon–Dark Matter Streaming Velocities
Supersonic baryon–dark-matter streaming velocities established after recombination influence the thermal and dynamical evolution of gas in the first halos and therefore the probability of forming direct-collapse seeds [9]. Streaming motions can suppress gas accretion and cooling, delaying Population III star formation and making some halos more conducive to isothermal collapse; models find that regions with strong streaming motions preferentially host massive-seed formation at high redshift [9]. The dossier reports substantial quantitative impacts from strong streaming: reductions in gas accretion rates by ~50%, suppression of H2 formation by ~70%, and increases in DCBH seed formation probability by ~300% in affected regions, leading to measurable modulation of high-redshift SMBH demographics and 21-cm/galaxy survey signatures [9]. These effects make the spatial distribution and timing of early SMBH seeds highly nonuniform and imprintable on observables of the epoch of reionization [9].

## Growth and Merger-driven Evolution
After initial formation by stellar collapse or direct collapse, black holes grow through gas accretion and mergers with other black holes and stellar systems; accretion can dominate mass growth especially in gas-rich environments [1,9]. SMBHs observed in galaxy centers can result from successive accretion episodes and hierarchical mergers—merging of smaller black holes and coalescence during galaxy mergers contributes significantly to the high masses seen at low redshift [1]. Simulations suggest that a non-negligible fraction (order 1–10% in some studies) of atomic-cooling halos at z = 10–20 could form direct-collapse seeds, with formation timescales for seeds often estimated around 10 Myr, implying a range of evolutionary pathways and timescales to build SMBHs [5]. The lifecycle of binary SMBHs (from galaxy merger to tight binary and finally coalescence via gravitational-wave emission) structures the long-term mass assembly and leaves observable signatures such as gravitational waves and dynamical imprints on host galaxies [1,5].

## Conclusion
Black holes form via multiple, distinct channels whose relative importance depends on mass scale, environment, and cosmic epoch. Stellar collapse reliably produces black holes above a few solar masses (minimum ≈2–4 M☉) and dominates the formation of stellar-mass black holes observed in the local universe [1]. Direct collapse of pristine gas in the early universe provides a viable route to form massive seeds (≳10^4 M☉) when H2 cooling is suppressed and accretion rates reach ∼0.1–1 M☉ yr^−1, but this route is rare and sensitive to radiative backgrounds and streaming velocities [9,5]. Baryon–dark-matter streaming motions and radiative environments therefore act as key modulators that set the spatial distribution, timing, and abundance of early SMBH seeds and thereby influence the population of SMBHs we observe at later times [9].

## Next Steps
Improved constraints require coordinated progress in simulations and observations focused on the epoch of reionization. Simulations should continue to refine the dependence of direct-collapse viability on Lyman–Werner and X-ray backgrounds, angular momentum transport, and the statistics of streaming velocities, building on semi-analytic and numerical results that report Jcrit thresholds and seed abundances [9,5]. Observational programs — including deep high-redshift spectroscopy (e.g., JWST) and 21-cm surveys — can test predictions about the abundance and spatial modulation of early SMBH seeds and their host galaxies, potentially discriminating regions with suppressed H2 cooling or strong streaming signatures [9]. Finally, combining gravitational-wave detections of black-hole mergers with electromagnetic surveys will constrain the relative contributions of stellar collapse, direct collapse, and merger-driven growth to the present-day SMBH population [1,5].
